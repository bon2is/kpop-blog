Jisoo of global sensation BLACKPINK stunned fans this week by recalling a moment early in her career when a simple hair color change landed her on a broadcast ban list. The revelation, shared during a recent interview with *Koreaboo*, shines a light on the strict visual standards that have long governed South Korea’s music shows.

## Background

BLACKPINK, formed by YG Entertainment in 2016, has become one of the most recognizable K‑pop acts worldwide. Comprising Jisoo, Jennie, Rosé, and Lisa, the quartet has amassed billions of YouTube views, charted on the Billboard Hot 100, and broken streaming records on Spotify and Apple Music. Their debut single “Whistle” topped the Gaon Digital Chart, while the 2022 album *Born Pink* entered the Billboard 200 at No. 1, cementing their status as a global powerhouse.

Before BLACKPINK, Jisoo trained for five years under YG, appearing in cameo roles and as a backup dancer for senior artists. Her calm stage presence and vocal stability quickly made her a fan favorite. In 2018, Jisoo released the solo track “‘Hope’” for the *SM Station* project, showcasing her versatility beyond group activities. Yet, like many idols, she has navigated the strict image regulations imposed by Korean broadcast networks such as KBS, MBC, and SBS.

The Korean entertainment industry has historically enforced a “clean‑image” policy, especially for music shows that reach family audiences. Regulations cover everything from clothing to tattoos, piercings, and even hair color. While the rules have softened over the past decade, they remain a potent force that can affect an artist’s promotion schedule.

## What Happened

During the *Koreaboo* interview, Jisoo recounted that in early 2017—just months after BLACKPINK’s debut—she experimented with a light‑blonde shade for a personal photoshoot. The new look, while fashionable, conflicted with the broadcast standards of major Korean music programs, which at the time prohibited “unnatural” hair colors for performers on live stages.

“After the photoshoot, I received a call from our agency saying I couldn’t appear on any music shows until I changed my hair back,” Jisoo explained. “It was a shock because I didn’t realize a hair color could become a career obstacle.” The ban lasted for two weeks, during which the group performed on pre‑recorded segments without Jisoo, prompting fans to rally on social media with the hashtag #JisooHairJustice.

YG Entertainment issued a brief statement, confirming the incident and noting that the company had quickly complied with the network’s request. The agency also highlighted that Jisoo reverted to her natural dark brown hair before the next live broadcast, allowing the group to resume full promotions for “Playing With Fire.”

Since then, Jisoo has occasionally experimented with subtle highlights, but she has avoided drastic color changes during active promotion cycles, adhering to the now‑more‑lenient guidelines that still require prior approval from broadcast stations.

## Why This Matters

Jisoo’s anecdote underscores the lingering tension between artistic expression and broadcast conformity in K‑pop. While Western markets celebrate bold fashion statements, Korean networks maintain a protective stance to preserve a “family‑friendly” image. This clash can directly impact an artist’s exposure, as music shows remain a primary platform for chart performance and fan engagement.

The incident also reflects a broader shift in industry policy. In recent years, K‑pop idols such as IU, Taeyeon, and even BLACKPINK members themselves have been allowed more creative freedom, with networks gradually relaxing hair‑color restrictions. However, Jisoo’s experience shows that the transition is not uniform, and older regulations can still surface unexpectedly.

From a commercial perspective, the brief ban could have affected BLACKPINK’s early chart momentum. Music‑show wins contribute to digital sales spikes and streaming boosts, which feed into Circle Chart rankings and even Billboard’s Global 200. Although the two‑week gap was quickly remedied, it serves as a reminder of how non‑musical factors can influence a group’s commercial trajectory.

## What Fans Can Expect

Following the interview, fans have been eager to see Jisoo push the envelope again. With BLACKPINK’s upcoming world tour slated for 2025, speculation abounds about whether she might debut a new hair style on stage. The group’s management has hinted at “fresh concepts” for the tour, but no official visual details have been released yet.

Additionally, Jisoo is set to drop her first full solo EP later this year, titled *Dreamscape*. While the release date is still under wraps, early teasers suggest a more mature aesthetic, possibly allowing her to revisit the blonde look under controlled conditions. The EP will be available on all major streaming platforms—Spotify, Apple Music, and Amazon Music—and physical copies will be sold through YG’s online store and select K‑pop retailers.

For those wondering where to watch BLACKPINK’s upcoming performances, the group’s concerts will be streamed live on YouTube’s official K‑pop channel and will later appear on the V‑Live app for replay. International fans can also catch the tour’s highlights on Netflix’s “K‑Pop Live” series, scheduled for release in early 2026.

## 💭 Editor's Take

💭 Jisoo’s hair‑color ban feels like a time capsule of K‑pop’s earlier, more rigid era. As someone who’s followed the industry since the first wave of Hallyu, I find it both fascinating and a little frustrating that a simple fashion choice could temporarily silence one of the world’s biggest idols. It’s a testament to how far the scene has come that today’s idols can experiment with bold colors on global stages without fearing a broadcast blackout.

That said, the story also highlights the importance of cultural context. While Western fans cheer on avant‑garde looks, Korean broadcasters still juggle public sentiment and advertiser expectations. Jisoo’s experience reminds us that K‑pop’s global dominance doesn’t erase its domestic roots. As the industry continues to evolve, I hope we see more transparent guidelines that let artists express themselves fully—hair, fashion, and all—while still respecting the diverse audiences they serve.
